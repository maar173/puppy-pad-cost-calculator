## How many pads you'll actually go through

Manufacturer and retailer guidance lands in the same place. In the first weeks of training, expect **three to six disposable pads a day**. That drops to one or two a day once your puppy starts holding its bladder longer, which happens at roughly one hour of control per month of age.

The working schedule most guides recommend: check the pad every 2 to 4 hours during the day and replace it overnight, or immediately after a heavy use. A 100-pack sounds enormous until you do the arithmetic — at four pads a day it's gone in about 25 days.

That's the reason per-pad price matters more than pack price. Over a four-month training window at four pads a day, you're buying somewhere around 480 pads.

## The layer stack: what separates a cheap pad from a good one

Training pads are a stack of layers, and each one does one job. When a pad fails, it's because one layer was skipped.

- **Quick-dry top sheet.** A non-woven surface that pulls liquid downward fast, so your puppy's paws stay dry and you get less tracking across the floor.
- **Absorbent core.** The important layer. Better pads use SAP (super absorbent polymer), which turns liquid into gel on contact, so a full pad doesn't slosh or seep back up. Budget pads often use two or three layers of fluff pulp instead, which saturates quickly.
- **Wetness-lock layer.** Sits under the core to stop backflow once the pad has absorbed.
- **Leak-proof backing.** A waterproof PE film running to sealed edges. This is the layer protecting your floor, and it's the one cheap pads thin out first. Pads described as "moisture-resistant" are not the same thing.
- **Optional extras.** A built-in attractant scent to guide the puppy to the same spot, and an activated carbon or charcoal layer that neutralises ammonia rather than masking it with fragrance. Heavy fragrance is a poor fit for puppies with skin sensitivities.

Layer counts on packaging run from two or three up to five or six. A five-layer build with a polymer core is the minimum worth paying for if you're leaving a pad down overnight or for more than four hours.

## Sizing: the mistake that costs the most pads

The rule is blunt and it's repeated across every serious guide: the pad has to be big enough for your dog to stand on with all four paws inside the surface. If paws are touching the floor before the job is done, you bought the wrong size, and no amount of training will fix it. A pad that's too small is the single most common reason dogs miss the edge.

## Making the pad work, so you buy fewer of them

Everything above is about cost. This part is about volume, and volume is where the real savings sit. Halving your daily pad count does more than any cashback rate ever will.

Put the pad in one fixed spot on a hard, wipeable floor, away from the bed, food, and water bowls. Take your puppy to it after waking, after meals, after play, and before bed. Use one short cue — "go potty" — and stand quietly rather than turning it into playtime. Reward within about two seconds of finishing, because the timing is what teaches the lesson. Clean misses with an enzyme cleaner, not a general purpose spray; leftover scent pulls the dog back to the same wrong spot.

If the pad slides, use a holder or tray, or pick a pad with adhesive corners. If your puppy shreds it, try a frame rather than a bigger pack of the same pad.

Most puppies are ready to start transitioning off pads around four to six months, once they reliably signal they need to go. Move the pad gradually toward the door and then outside instead of removing it abruptly. For apartment dwellers and high-rise residents, pads aren't a training crutch so much as a practical necessity — asking an eight-week-old puppy to hold it until the lift arrives isn't a plan.

## Quick answers

**Disposable or washable?** Disposable pads are the right call during the messy early weeks, for travel, and for households where laundry is already a bottleneck. Washable pads cost more upfront and cut both long-term spend and waste, provided you wash and fully dry them promptly. They're a decent backup layer under a crate or beside the door.

**Can you use them for adult or senior dogs?** Yes, and this is where the larger sizes earn their price. Look for a leak-proof backing and a pad big enough for the dog to stand on comfortably.

**How often do you need to change one?** As soon as it's well used. A fresh, dry surface is what keeps your dog coming back to the same spot, and a soaked pad is the second most common cause of misses after a pad that's too small.
